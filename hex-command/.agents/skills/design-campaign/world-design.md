# World and campaign design

For the author and stage generators.

## Spirit

The world of the game is and should be grounded in medieval fantasy (unless asked otherwise).
It is not a pleasant world to live in, with heroes who sometimes make you wish they had never existed.
If not for the villains, because they are worse.
Forced labor doesn't exist in this world, because it's just called work.
Villagers work for their lords; slaves work for their owners.
The legal system is very involved, and by that I mean it involves people who can force each other into their truths by killing them or threatening to kill them.
There are, of course, light places in the world, like the palaces of the nobility, where nobles sometimes get to imagine a better world and, in the best case, get laughed at, or, more usually, get killed by other nobles for a slice of their pie and authority.
As I mentioned, you do see heroes winking into existence from time to time who, in their righteousness, will burn a village or two to get to their villain or to tell people the truth, which is why normal people try to stay away from them.

It's a world of wizards, tyrants, cults and barbaric tribes.
It's a world of magic and curses, and a world of fire and blood.
Be careful where you place your foot, because you might be lucky to get it cut off instead of having your neck stuck in a noose.

## Races

### Humans

The most boring race there is; they adapt to every place, every lifestyle and every battlefield.
Having strong cavalry and infantry, they dominate the open plains.
And other people, of course.
There is not much to say about humans, but don't worry, they'll tell you about themselves.

### Elves

They prefer to live in peace with nature, which gives them a long time to contemplate and remember.
Usually bad things, since even they can't escape how memory works.
They don't like strangers, even among their own kind, so they are good with all things that help them maintain distance, be it spears or arrows.

### Dwarves

If elves love nature, dwarves absolutely fuck it.
There probably were forest dwarves a long time ago, but they cut them all down and went extinct.
The only kinds of dwarves left that way are the ones that live in mountains or under the mountains, since consuming all the rocks is a bit harder.

Interacting with stones so much has made them equally tough, and so they have better armor, yet equally better crushing weapons against that armor.
Even their magic is built upon scratching stones to make runes.
When that doesn't work, they will scratch you with their fire-powder guns, jumping over the hills with their goats.

### Woodland folk

Led by mysterious Wildkeepers, these beasts realized that the best way to keep humans away from their groves is to organize kill zones and make them pay dearly for their crime, that crime usually being existing.
While other races forge their shiny plates from metal, these nasty dwellers decided to show everyone how to be real pricks and turn themselves into thorns, making you think twice before approaching their settlements.
If you do, you will be happy to be killed by a wolf, since being torn apart by a living tree is usually worse.

### Skeletons

There are those who crave to never age a day and live forever.
And this is not hard to do, as it turns out, if not for one complication: living bodies.
These things are absolutely uncomfortable in their constant change.
Liches solve this problem by getting rid of them.
Yes, it means that you also lose your soul and all that is good in life, but then do you need it all that much?
Their minions don't think about that much, eventually turning into machines with black faces, for thinking is redundant when there is a lich who will tell everyone how to behave.

Living beings avoid them and almost never interact with them, but that's not a problem for a lich.
Living beings die.
(Usually because they kill each other, but that process can be helped.)
And when they die, the master can get themselves new slaves.

### Zombies

If skeletons don't have many friends, then zombies... well.
You have seen them.
Their own parents will be the first to burn their children away if they step into their houses.
And rightfully so, for if they came to you, at best they want to kill you... before they eat you.
Humans, of course, will frequently do much worse things to you, but, you know, the looks.

A true testament to a world where death is so common that some of them decided to try to walk away.
Of course, soon after that, their brains turn to mush, but they show that you don't need brains to function.
Having legs, hands and stomachs is enough, and eating more will grant them powers.
The main goal of a zombie is to get bigger and more hideous.

## Principles

- **Consistency.** Preserve established facts across the campaign. Changes must follow from events in the story.
- Develop original characters, conflicts and scenes. Use references and sampled ingredients for structural patterns.

## Enemy and protagonist

For enemy organization and presentation, read the [opposition distribution](reference/opposition_distribution.md); for enemy pursuits, read the [motive distribution](reference/motive_distribution.md). For player-character backgrounds, factions and pursuits, read the [protagonist distribution](reference/protagonist_distribution.md).

Enemies need not be tragic, complicated or justified. They may want crowns, territory, artifacts, magical power, revenge, victims or dominance. Some are straightforward predators. Others believe in their undertaking.

Give the enemy a concrete undertaking and forces capable of pursuing it. Establish the leader's desires, plans and how they command their faction. Develop the protagonist's own pursuit against that selected enemy. Protagonists themselves can be dangerous aggressors.

Design the characters and factions the story needs. Establish their motivations, roles and responsibilities: what they want, what part they play, and what they are answerable for. Establish faction relationships, feelings toward one another and the balance of power behind the conflict.

## Chapters

Each chapter is a battle. The characters enter it pursuing a goal and confronting a problem. Its outcome changes their situation and moves the story forward. Use the supplied story kind and definition to shape that development, and the supplied format to present the candidate.

The first chapter hooks the player through something happening that needs resolution, without explaining everything. Establish the mood, a believable immediate goal, and a real problem.

Derive each subsequent chapter from the previous chapter's achievement: what it enables, how the factions respond, and what the characters now pursue.

Establish what the player commands and what makes the battle a particular tactical problem: opposing forces, terrain, recruitment locations, available gold, recruitment and veterans. Give each achievement a concrete benefit or change for the player.

The campaign must offer something new to play with: a tactic to discover, units with unfamiliar strengths or weaknesses, a capability to acquire, or an interaction between forces and terrain. Develop battles that let the player discover and exploit it.

Develop events from the characters' actions and decisions. Cause and effect must make internal sense, even when the explanation is initially withheld from the player. Both sides need clear motivations to kill or seriously injure each other.

## Alternatives and forks

Alternative finishing objectives provide different ways to complete a battle. Optional encounters can also have alternative outcomes. Neither automatically constitutes a story fork.

Use **forks** for story decisions enacted through events. Choose the point during the chapter when it becomes a fork. Show the shared situation and each branch's objectives, achieved results and playable consequences. Each option changes how the story unfolds and what the player faces or can do afterward; branches may reunite. Each branch must change what the player actually plays in one or more chapters, beginning in the current or immediately following chapter. They may also lead to entirely different chapters. A campaign can have no fork.

A forced "pick one, lose another" is not a fork; it is bullshit.

For examples of fork frequency, placement and gameplay effects, read the [fork distribution](reference/fork_distribution.md).

## Finale

The finale:

- Ties the previous chapters' developments together.
- Achieves the protagonist's original goal, or the replacement goal established by a chapter with a **Fundamental realignment** ingredient.
- Resolves the buildup from the preceding chapters. This usually involves killing the villain, but that is only part of the resolution.

The finale can be planned at the outset or developed alongside the preceding chapters.

The supplied finale kind describes the battle's story development; the ending describes what follows it. Both must fit the campaign's established goal and events.

## Points of interest

A **point of interest** is a place on the map with small optional content: a structure, a hidden unique enemy, or another encounter. Completing or exploring it gives a gameplay reward: an item, effect, unit or discovered clue that helps with the main task. Establish the reward's actual use in play; an item needs a gameplay effect, not merely a name.

## Events

Linked events establish, enact or resolve objectives. Independent developments change the situation outside task execution or resolution. Optional encounters connect optional actions with their consequences and rewards.

Keep connected effects together and distinguish incompatible outcomes. Ground conditions in the new situation. A group of related event kinds can describe one scene; its listing does not prescribe chronology. Distinguish actual decisions from automatic condition checks.

## Objectives

For the relative frequency of objective kinds and their roles, read the [objective distribution](reference/objective_distribution.md).

A gameplay objective puts the story into concrete tasks units can perform. Use the supplied objective kinds and give each task a target and completion condition. Follow the supplied sequence, AND requirements and OR alternatives. Tasks can be intermediate steps, required parts, alternative ways to finish, or optional content. Defeating an enemy ends the chapter only when its victory requirements are fulfilled.

Objectives describe intended goals, not guaranteed facts about the world or literal accounts of their resolution.

## Unit references

A unit roster lists units referencing existing JSON definitions, existing
artwork files, or new units with their capabilities. Social roles such as
"household soldier" and character titles such as "bailiff" do not identify
a unit type.

State each unit's level. Prefer L1 unless there is an explicit reason for a
higher level. Existing units' levels are stated in their artwork references
and/or unit JSON definitions.

When choosing units and developing their gameplay, each of these checks is mandatory for both the author and generators:

- Read the [game definitions](../../../rust/data/game/units/) for implemented unit rules.
- Read the relevant [visual lineups](../../../pixel/art/units/lineups/) for the full roster and promotion branches.
- View the relevant [unit artwork](../../../pixel/art/units/), including higher-level forms, with an image-viewing tool. Reading descriptions or lineup files does not replace viewing the pictures.
- Inspect the available building blocks: [unit rules, attacks, equipment and advancement](../../../rust/catalog/src/schema/units.rs), [perception](../../../rust/catalog/src/schema/perception.rs), [scoped effects](../../../rust/catalog/src/schema/effects.rs), and [event actions](../../../rust/catalog/src/schema/events.rs).

Use the artwork and roster to explore what these mechanics could make fun: combat roles, abilities, progression, enemies and interactions between them. Propose definitions for pictured units using supported mechanics; identify additional mechanics when the concept needs them. An absent unit definition does not itself require engine changes.

Complete this exploration before selecting forces or defining the faction's portrayal.
